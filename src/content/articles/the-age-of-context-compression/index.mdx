Every morning at **7:50 a.m.**, my phone sends me an **AI joke**.

Not a regular joke. Not a quote. Not a weather update pretending to be a personality. An actual **AI joke**, delivered to me before coffee, before sunlight has fully reached my frontal lobe, and before I have had any real chance of becoming a reasonable citizen again. And the most ridiculous part is not that this is happening. The most ridiculous part is that **I asked for it**.

I built this. I set it up. I curated the timing. I trained the inputs. I basically turned my phone into an overly attentive intern with a sense of humor and a calendar problem. Somewhere along the way, I stopped just using technology and started **orchestrating my own reality through it**. That is what I cannot stop thinking about.

Because when I step back and really look at how I move through a normal day, it is almost absurd how p**ersonalized and compressed everything has become.** I have different schedules for different parts of the day. I selectively decide when and how I want information delivered to me. I ingest updates in formats I prefer, at the times I prefer, in the tone I can tolerate, depending on whether it is 8 a.m. or 8 p.m. I filter what matters, suppress what does not, summarize what is too long, and reroute what I do not want to think about yet. I have, quite literally, built a custom information ecosystem around my own brain. And I know I am not alone.

I have created GPTs for my sick parents to make things simpler, gentler, and more personalized for them. I have watched myself use AI to do in two hours what used to take me weeks, sometimes months. I used to make books for my niece the old-fashioned way, lovingly and slowly, like a tiny, sleep-deprived publishing house. Now I can still make them lovingly, but much faster, with more room to iterate, refine, and personalize. The care is still there. The compression is what changed. ***That, to me, is the real story of this moment.***

We keep talking about AI as if the only question is whether it will replace jobs, write better emails, or make meetings slightly less painful. Meanwhile, something much bigger is happening in plain sight:

### **We are redesigning the distance between intention and action.**

We are living through a period in which **the distance between wanting and getting, asking and knowing, discovering and buying is collapsing**. The amount of friction between intention and action is shrinking almost everywhere. That is the shift. And from where I sit in commerce, I believe it is one of the most important shifts we need to understand.

This is not just about attention spans, even though that is usually where the conversation starts. I do not actually think people have lost the ability to focus. I think people focus very intensely on things that feel relevant, immediate, personalized, and rewarding. That is different. It is not the death of attention. It is **the rewiring of expectation**.

We are being trained by systems that make progress feel instant. Search is faster. Answers are faster. Content is faster. Decision-making is faster. Even our boredom barely survives anymore. The second there is an empty moment, we fill it. We check something, prompt something, listen to something, summarize something, optimize something. We are not just living with more technology. We are living with **less patience for anything that does not immediately meet us where we are**.

I see this everywhere.

I see it in my nephew Damien. I see it in my niece Ella. I see it in my aging friends, who may not want every new app, but absolutely expect things to be easier than they were five years ago. I see it in my friends who love technology and in my friends who claim they hate it while asking their phone for directions, restaurant recommendations, weather forecasts, and real-time flight updates like that is not also a relationship with technology.

On paper, all of these people should be in completely different silos. Different generations. Different preferences. Different levels of comfort. But they are not in silos anymore, because the same environment is shaping all of them. We are all adapting to tools that reduce effort, preserve context, and anticipate need.

That is why this matters so much beyond technology itself. **Context compression is changing how people communicate, how they learn, how they interact, and, naturally, how they shop.**

Communication is becoming more conversational and less procedural. People do not want to learn the architecture of every interface. They want to express intent and be understood. Learning is becoming more adaptive and less linear. People do not necessarily want the full manual before they begin. They want to ask, refine, test, and keep moving. Interaction is becoming more fluid, because systems are no longer just static destinations. They are becoming responsive participants. They remember. They suggest. They predict. Increasingly, they act. **So of course commerce is changing too.**

And if we are honest, it would have been stranger if it had not.

For years, digital commerce trained customers to navigate. Type in the search bar. Choose from a menu. Filter by size. Sort by price. Open six tabs. Compare three products. Forget why you started. Get distracted. Buy socks instead. It worked, mostly, because it was the best model available at the time. But it was always asking the customer to do a lot of translation work.

***Now that translation work is starting to move into the system.***

This is where I think so many people are underestimating what is actually happening. What we call **agentic commerce** sounds futuristic, slightly intimidating, and honestly a little too polished as a phrase. But when you strip away the jargon, it is simply the next stage of this shift. It is commerce that helps. Commerce that interprets. Commerce that carries intent. Commerce that can increasingly take action alongside the customer, and sometimes on the customer’s behalf. **That means shopping will feel less like navigating a store and more like continuing a conversation.**

A customer will not just search for “black dress.” She will say, “I need something for a fall wedding, not too formal, can be worn again, preferably flattering but easy.” Or, “I need jeans for a trip, good on planes, polished enough for dinner, not stiff.” Or, “I want a gift for my brother that says I tried, but not too hard.” That is not a search behavior problem. That is a human behavior reality. **And the commerce systems that win will be the ones that understand what people mean, not just what they type.**

This is already beginning. Discovery is moving into chat interfaces, publisher ecosystems, recommendation layers, social platforms, digital assistants, and new surfaces that do not always look like commerce until a purchase suddenly happens there. **The storefront is no longer the only place the shopping journey lives.** In some cases, it is not even where the journey starts. As someone who works in commerce, I do not see this as a novelty. I see it as a structural shift.

And yes, I also see the panic. Every time a new tool shows up, half the internet acts like we will all be replaced by lunchtime. Then, twenty minutes later, those same people are using it to summarize a document, write a brief, plan a trip, or organize a meeting no one wanted in the first place. **We are in this strange phase where we are both terrified and deeply attached to our own productivity gains.** I understand that tension because I live it too.

I used to make books for my niece that would take me months. Real months. Writing, revising, formatting, designing, obsessing, reworking, and generally behaving like the world’s most underpaid boutique publishing house. Now I can do something equally thoughtful in two hours. Not because I care less. Because the tools have changed. And if I am being honest, I would be ridiculous to pretend I have not changed with them. We all have.

That does not mean all change is good. It does mean pretending nothing is changing is not a strategy. For commerce leaders, the job now is not to romanticize the old funnel. It is to understand what the customer is becoming accustomed to, and then build responsibly for that future. That is why I keep coming back to loyalty. Because the real risk in this next era is not simply that technology changes the buying journey. Technology always changes the buying journey.

### **The real risk is that the relationship between brand and customer gets abstracted one layer further away, and brands wake up too late to realize that the interface now owns the intimacy.**

That should concern all of us. If customers begin discovering, evaluating, and transacting across assistant-led or agent-mediated environments, then loyalty cannot stay trapped inside one owned channel. It cannot be something that only functions if the customer comes back through the exact same front door every time. In a world of context compression, **loyalty has to become portable**. **By that I mean this: wherever the customer is, our ability to care for them has to travel with them**.

If they discover us through an AI assistant, we should still be able to serve them well. If they start their journey through a publisher, we should still be able to recognize value in that relationship. If they are loyal to us, that loyalty should still matter when the interface changes. The customer should not lose the benefits of trust, recognition, relevance, and service just because commerce has become more distributed.

That is a design problem. A systems problem. A strategy problem. And most of all, **a leadership problem**. **It is our responsibility to adapt. It is our responsibility to build for this world before someone else builds it for us.**

**It is our responsibility to ensure that as the path to purchase becomes more compressed, more conversational, and more agentic, the customer experience becomes more human, not less.**

That means building systems that preserve trust. Building experiences that understand intent. Building commerce that works across channels without losing brand integrity. Building loyalty models that are resilient enough to travel. Building governance that keeps innovation from becoming chaos. Building with enough humility to admit that the customer is changing faster than our org charts are.

Because that, ultimately, is where this is going.

We are moving toward a world in which context follows the customer more fluidly than ever before. Shopping will be embedded in more places. Discovery will happen in more surfaces. Recommendations will become more dynamic. Interfaces will become more conversational. Assistance will become more intelligent. And the brands that win will not simply be the brands with the best products or the loudest media spend. They will be the brands that understand the behavioral shift underneath all of it.

### **That shift is context compression. It is the collapse of distance between intention and action.**

And once you see it, you start to realize it is not just changing commerce. It is changing people. Which means, whether we feel ready or not, it is changing the job of everyone building the future of commerce too.
